EAL Programmers

Barriers to Learning

EAL programmers did not tend to struggle with the actual ‘grammar’ of code - the structure of many programming languages follows a logic based, almost mathematical quality:

//if condition = true, then do this

It is the intersection between natural language and programming syntax that presents the most barriers when learning to code.

96% of respondents read programming-related materials in English. 34% of respondents say that these English instructional materials were difficult to read (including textbooks, online tutorials, forums, and documentation.) Those that were in their native languages are out of date, badly translated or even non-existent.

Programming resources use more technical jargon, is not found frequently in everyday life, such as ‘concatenate’, which is the process of joining multiple objects simultaneously.

Everyday words that are repurposed for programming concepts may have different meanings within code, such as the regular word ‘class’ versus a ‘class’ within the context of object oriented programming. A native English speaker may immediately make the connection with one of the regular english definitions, “a group into which goods, services, or people are put according to their standard”, and its similar role within code - a blueprint to create similar objects - but an EAL programmer may mistakenly initially connect it with a ‘class’ within a school. This paper argues this creates a form of semantic interference - the two competing meanings require even more processing and memory resources. Constructed keywords (those without an equivalent in everyday language) were recognised more accurately by EAL students compared to repurposed words, such as class, but EAL students were found to have a higher intrinsic cognitive load overall compared to their ENL classmates within a Python keyword identification task. EAL students report a significantly higher linguistic burden when coding. [cite]

Programming syntax itself, as we have seen, is english-based - where a fluent English speaker makes one ‘translation’ from their logic to English-based code, an ESL programmer must make multiple ‘jumps’ - translating a problem from English to their native language, then their solution into English-based syntax. Additionally, abbreviations used for variables and functions (ie, NumList for a numberedList) are not as obvious for EAL programmers. This gets worse as identifiers get longer and more complex.
