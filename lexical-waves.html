Latin's Influence on the English Language

Lexical "Waves"

Latin’s influence on the English language is primarily lexical, with many words derived from Latin roots. Words were loaned in multiple ‘waves’ throughout its development as a language, detailed below:

Old English

Initial loan words - prior to the invasion and settlement of Britain - can be categorised into a few themes:

War (camp, from Latin campus, staet from strata)
Trade (such as win, from Latin vinum)
Domestic Life (cuppe, from Latin cuppa)
and some foods.

The Christianising of Britain also brought about new vocabulary, from the 7th to 10th centuries. Loan words from this period relate to religion and learning, such as altar and verse

Middle English

The Norman Invasion in 1066: The Normans spoke Norman French (French influenced by a Germanic dialect). Common people spoke English, while the Aristocrats spoke Norman French. The resulting mesh of these two languages is called Middle English.

By the 13th century, about 10,000 French words were loaned into English, many relating to the realms of court, administration, law, the church, and art. French is a romance language - a direct decendant of Latin. Words such as prince, from latin's 'prīnceps', parliament, from french 'parler', and science, from Latin 'scientia' were loaned during this period.

It also brought about significant changes to the spelling of words, where English words took on French spelling conventions: cwen → queen.

Modern English

The Renaissance: a time known for rapid learning and discovery, resulted in even more words taken from Latin and Greek to describe new concepts and ideas. Many prefixes and suffixes in the scientific world are borrowed directly from Latin, creating words like hypothermia. Many of the abstract and technical words introduced during this period later became suitable for describing computational concepts. As a result, modern programming vocabulary contains a significant number of terms whose ultimate origins are this lexical influx.
